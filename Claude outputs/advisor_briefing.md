## 2. What changed this week

### New result: 57.8 of 100 points are identical for all 96 repositories

Our FAIR-R rubric checks fifteen things. Nine of them gave exactly the same answer for every repository in the corpus — every repo lacks a landing page, a data licence, a workflow language, and so on. A question everyone answers identically cannot distinguish anyone, so more than half the scoring system was doing no work. Only three criteria vary enough to be usable, and one of them — does the code carry a software licence — accounts for almost all the predictive power we reported.

This explains the null result rather than excusing it. The aggregate score failed to predict reproduction because the rubric was designed for curated data archives and we applied it to working code repositories, where most of its questions are automatically "no".

Two consequences worth your attention. First, with the model specified properly and corrected for multiple comparisons, the software-licence effect now *survives* statistical correction for both outcomes — the finding we reported is defensible against the objection I most expected from reviewers. Second, re-weighting the rubric does not rescue it: a single bit of information, "does this repository have a licence", predicts as well as the entire instrument.

### A defect in our released code, found and fixed

The results files in the public repository did not reproduce Table 5 of the accepted paper — they were generated by an older script using a stricter matching setting, and nothing recorded that. Anyone downloading our artifact would have obtained roughly half our headline number with no explanation. The published table is correct and I have verified it reproduces exactly; the corrected files are ready to commit. For a paper about reproducibility this was worth catching before a reader did.

## 3. Decisions

My own leaning is marked on each, but these are yours to direct — particularly the first three, which are hard to reverse.

### D1 — Do we submit the workshop paper today?

Submitting costs us something real: it turns the execution-recipe work, the reproduction engine and the failure taxonomy into *prior work*, which we must then exceed in the journal rather than count as new. That removes roughly 35–40 points of our novelty budget.

Against that: it is a nearly finished paper, an indexed publication, a presentation slot at a conference we are attending, and it establishes priority on the ExecutionRecipe term.

> **My recommendation.** Submit. We can meet the novelty requirement without that material — the new analysis above plus seven further components already exceed it. Submission also stays reversible until camera-ready on 30 September, so we keep the option open while we resolve D2.

### D2 — Should we ask the special-issue editors whether one submission may extend both papers?

If the invitation covers only the main-track paper, the workshop material cannot be folded in — which changes the journal's structure substantially. The answer would arrive well before the 30 September camera-ready, so we would still have room to act on it.

You may have a faster route to this than I do: the conference is in Bangkok and organised through AIAT.

> **My recommendation.** Ask this week. It is the single largest unknown in the plan, and one email settles it.

**Needs you — you likely know the organisers.**

### D3 — Do we reframe the journal paper around the new finding?

Two options. We can write a conventional extension — the measurement paper plus the reproduction work, combined and expanded. Or we can lead with the new claim: that reproducibility scores measure how well a study is *described* rather than whether it *reproduces*, show why FAIR-style rubrics break down on executable research code, and identify the few signals that do carry information.

The second is a claim about the assessment literature, not only about our own tool. It is more ambitious and more exposed.

> **My recommendation.** Reframe. A merger of two published papers invites a reviewer to see two-thirds familiar material; a paper with a genuinely new thesis does not. Both prior papers then serve as evidence for it.

### D4 — Which new components should I prioritise?

Eight are planned; two are essential and the rest are tradeable against the December 2027 deadline. In my order of value:

- **Essential — the criterion analysis.** Done this week. It is the thesis.
- **Essential — frontier-model extraction.** Re-run our extraction with a commercial model to show our conclusions are not an artefact of using a small free one.
- **High value — other instruments.** Run the same audit on F-UJI and FAIRshake. If they collapse the same way, the finding generalises beyond us.
- **Useful — selective verification.** Use the signals that work to decide which studies are worth verifying, and report the effort saved. This is close to what Prof. Fujiwara suggested could anchor the thesis.
- **Useful — semantic diff, cross-level linkage, taxonomy validation.**

> **My recommendation.** Lock the two essentials, then take the others in the order above as time allows. Dropping any two still leaves us above the novelty threshold.

### D5 — May I have approximately 35 USD of API budget?

This funds the frontier-model comparison — one of the two essential components. It answers the sharpest question a reviewer can ask: when we say information is missing from these repositories, do we actually mean our cheap model failed to find it?

I should flag the risk honestly: if the commercial model performs much better, our claim weakens from "the information is missing" to "our extractor was weak". I intend to report whichever result we get — with the re-analysis attached, both outcomes are publishable, but only if we decide that in advance.

> **My recommendation.** Approve. The cost is trivial and the experiment is not optional; a reviewer will ask for it regardless.

**Needs you — budget approval.**

Our failure taxonomy was assigned by a program. Journal reviewers will expect two people to have categorised the same failures independently, so we can report how often they agreed. Roughly twenty-six cases; perhaps half a day of someone's time.

This is the only component I cannot complete alone, so it needs scheduling early rather than late.

> **My recommendation.** A labmate familiar with ML repositories would be sufficient — it does not need a co-author.
