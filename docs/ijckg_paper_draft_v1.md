# SRAF: A Standards-Grounded FAIR-R Score for Auditing the Reproducibility of Machine-Learning Research

*Suhel Acharya, Chutiporn Anutariya, Yasuyuki Minamiyama, Hideaki Takeda, Vilas Wuwongse*
*(first draft — v1)*

## Abstract

Computational reproducibility remains a persistent problem in machine-learning research, yet it is seldom measured in a principled, standards-grounded way. Existing FAIR assessments target datasets rather than the executable artifacts, namely code, software environments, and reported results, on which machine-learning claims depend. We present SRAF, a Semantic Reproducibility Auditing Framework that formulates reproducibility as a measurement problem over a research-provenance knowledge graph. Building on the RDIP integration profile, SRAF lifts each study into a per-study graph that fuses deterministic repository parsing with large-language-model extraction of metadata from the paper, and it scores each study with FAIR-R, a graded, maturity-based instrument whose weights are derived from the RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model, F-UJI, FAIR4RS, and the ML Reproducibility Checklist, and which extends FAIR with a dedicated Reproducible dimension operationalised as executable SHACL shapes. We apply SRAF to a corpus of 96 machine-learning repositories, reconstructing each declared software environment on a containerless high-performance-computing cluster and recording a two-level outcome of dependency resolution and full build. We find that 55.8% of repositories resolve and 52.3% build, that 26 are fully reproducible from clone to build, and that the presence of a software license is a significant predictor of successful reproduction (odds ratio ≈ 7.3, p ≈ 0.025). Against a human-verified ground truth, we further characterise the accuracy and limits of automated metadata extraction and relate the FAIR-R score to observed reproduction outcomes. SRAF and its annotated corpus provide a reusable, standards-grounded instrument for auditing, and for helping authors improve, the reproducibility of computational research.

**Keywords:** research knowledge graph · computational reproducibility · FAIR principles · reproducibility metadata · large-language-model extraction

## 1  Introduction

The scientific community continues to face a reproducibility crisis. Surveys report that a majority of researchers have failed to reproduce another group's results, and empirical studies of computational research repeatedly find that published artifacts lack the information required for independent reproduction. In machine learning this problem is acute: a result depends not only on a dataset but on a specific software stack, a computing environment, a set of hyperparameters and random seeds, and a chain of activities relating them. These elements are typically described in prose, scattered across a paper and a code repository, and are rarely captured in a form that can be inspected, compared, or audited automatically.

The FAIR principles offer a framework for making research outputs Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable, and a mature ecosystem of assessment tools has grown around them. However, these tools were designed to evaluate datasets and their metadata records; they do not assess whether the *executable* artifacts of a computational study, its code, environment, and reported numbers, are complete enough to reconstruct the work. As a result, there is no widely adopted, standards-grounded way to measure the reproducibility readiness of a machine-learning artifact, or to ask empirically which metadata elements actually predict whether a study can be reproduced.

We address this gap with SRAF, a Semantic Reproducibility Auditing Framework. SRAF treats reproducibility as a measurement problem defined over a research-provenance knowledge graph. It builds on RDIP, a recently introduced project-centric integration profile that composes established vocabularies around a central research-project hub and adds reproducibility-oriented constructs [RDIP]. Whereas RDIP defines *what* a valid description of a study must contain, SRAF defines *how well* a study is described and *whether that description matters* for reproduction. Concretely, SRAF (i) constructs a per-study knowledge graph by fusing deterministic repository parsing with large-language-model extraction from the paper; (ii) scores each study with FAIR-R, a graded, standards-grounded instrument that extends FAIR with a dedicated Reproducible dimension and is operationalised as executable SHACL shapes; and (iii) validates the instrument against reproduction outcomes obtained by actually rebuilding each study's environment.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce **FAIR-R**, a graded, maturity-based reproducibility-scoring instrument whose criteria and weights are traceable to established standards, the RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model, F-UJI, FAIR4RS, and the ML Reproducibility Checklist, rather than assigned ad hoc. Second, we present a **large-scale empirical study** over 96 machine-learning repositories in which each declared environment is reconstructed on a compute cluster, yielding a two-level reproduction outcome and a logistic analysis of which metadata predict failure. Third, we provide a **human-verified benchmark and analysis** relating automated extraction accuracy and the FAIR-R score to observed reproducibility. Beyond the study, SRAF supports a concrete use case: an author, before submission, can lift a paper and its repository into the graph and receive a reproducibility score together with prioritised, actionable recommendations.

The ontology on which SRAF builds is the subject of a companion paper [RDIP]; here we treat it as a fixed target schema and focus on the scoring instrument, the empirical study, and their validation.

## 2  Related Work

Two lines of work are relevant. The first is the empirical study of computational reproducibility. Large surveys and artifact-evaluation programmes have documented that a substantial fraction of computational results cannot be independently reproduced, and community checklists, most prominently the ML Reproducibility Checklist and the ACM Artifact Review and Badging policy, enumerate the information an artifact should provide. These efforts establish *what good practice looks like* but stop short of an automated, quantitative instrument that measures how closely a given artifact approaches it, or of an empirical account of which elements causally matter for reproduction.

The second line is FAIR assessment. The RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model provides an authoritative set of indicators, each assigned an Essential / Important / Useful priority, and F-UJI operationalises many of them as an automated, maturity-scored tool. FAIR4RS extends the FAIR principles to research software, addressing executability, dependencies, and versioning. These frameworks are the natural foundation for a reproducibility instrument, but as published they target datasets and their metadata records rather than the executable artifacts and reported results of a computational study, and they do not include a dimension dedicated to computational reproduction. SRAF's FAIR-R score is designed to inherit the rigour of these standards, their indicator sets, priority weighting, and graded maturity scoring, while extending them with a Reproducible dimension grounded in the reproducibility checklists.

Finally, the representation layer that SRAF audits is the RDIP integration profile [RDIP], which composes VIVO, DCAT, PROV-O, BIBO, and CiTO around a research-project hub and adds constructs for software dependencies, computing environments, hyperparameters and random seeds, and evaluation results. RDIP contributes the schema; SRAF contributes the measurement instrument and the empirical study built upon it.

## 3  The SRAF Framework

SRAF operates in three stages: knowledge-graph construction, FAIR-R scoring, and outcome-based validation.

**Knowledge-graph construction.** For each study, SRAF produces a single named RDIP graph by combining two complementary sources. Repository-derived facts, software dependencies and version constraints, container and environment specifications, random seeds appearing in code, the repository license, and structural identifiers, are obtained deterministically by parsing the repository, and are therefore high-precision. Paper-derived facts, the methods, hyperparameters, datasets, and reported evaluation results described in the article, are obtained by a retrieval-augmented extraction pipeline that prompts a large language model to emit RDIP-typed metadata. We run three open-weight models served locally, so that the extraction step is itself reproducible and free of dependence on proprietary services. This deterministic-plus-model fusion reflects a deliberate division of labour: the repository supplies what can be parsed exactly, and the model supplies what exists only in prose.

**FAIR-R scoring.** Given a study's graph, FAIR-R computes a score in five dimensions: Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable, aligned to the RDA indicators, together with a novel Reproducible dimension that has no counterpart in dataset-oriented FAIR. The dimensions carry weights of 15, 15, 20, 20, and 30 out of 100, placing the largest mass on reproducibility. Two design choices distinguish FAIR-R from ad-hoc scorers. First, scoring is *graded* rather than binary: each criterion is evaluated at one of three maturity levels, absent, present but unstructured, or present and machine-readable, following the maturity-based philosophy of F-UJI and the RDA model, so that the quality of metadata, not merely its presence, is rewarded. Second, weights are *derived from standards* rather than assigned: within each dimension a criterion's share is proportional to the priority, Essential, Important, or Useful, of the corresponding RDA indicator. The Reproducible dimension is decomposed into computational environment, methodological transparency, and data provenance, each criterion citing a specific item of the ML Reproducibility Checklist or FAIR4RS. Every criterion is expressed as an executable SHACL shape, so that scoring is a validation over the graph rather than bespoke code, and the same shapes double as a conflict detector when two studies are compared.

**Validation.** Because a completeness score is only useful if it relates to real outcomes, SRAF is validated against reproduction outcomes obtained by actually reconstructing each study's environment (Section 5). We additionally calibrate the instrument against a small set of exemplary, independently reproduced artifacts that should sit at the top of the scale.

## 4  Corpus and Experimental Setup

Our corpus comprises 96 machine-learning repositories with associated papers, drawn from the same collection used to evaluate RDIP [RDIP]; 95 have retrievable papers. For each repository we attempt to reconstruct the declared software environment on a containerless high-performance-computing cluster using a streaming harness that clones, processes, and discards each repository in turn so that the corpus never accumulates on disk. Crucially, reconstruction is *version-aware*: rather than testing every repository against a single modern interpreter, each is built on the Python version it declares. We record a two-level outcome, whether dependencies *resolve* and whether the project *builds*, which serves as the dependent variable in the predictive analysis.

For metadata extraction we use three open-weight instruction-tuned models of increasing size, served locally through a standard inference server. Extraction accuracy is measured against a human-verified ground truth constructed by a strong model and then checked by hand, comprising a thoroughly verified gold set of 12 studies and a broader silver set of 95. We report entity-level precision, recall, and F1 on the reproducibility fields, using entity-aware matching that credits correct extractions expressed with different surface forms. Finally, we assemble a small reference set of five exemplary artifacts, peer-reviewed computational reproductions archived with persistent identifiers and open-licensed code, as high-scoring calibration anchors.

## 5  Results

**Reproduction is difficult, and version awareness matters.** Across the 96 repositories, 55.8% resolve their declared dependencies and 52.3% build successfully; only 26 studies (27%) are fully reproducible from clone to build. The version-aware design is consequential: testing every repository against a single modern interpreter understated the build rate, which rose from roughly 43% to 55.8% once each repository was matched to its declared interpreter, a more honest figure that isolates genuine metadata gaps from an artificial version mismatch. We also observe that about one repository in four places its environment files in subdirectories rather than at the root, so a naive automated check would miss them; careful, recursive artifact discovery is required.

**A software license predicts reproducibility.** Fitting a logistic regression of the build outcome on metadata predictors, the presence of a software license emerges as a significant predictor of successful reproduction (odds ratio ≈ 7.3, p ≈ 0.025). We interpret the license not as a causal factor in building software but as a signal of a generally well-maintained project: repositories that take the elementary step of declaring a license are markedly more likely to also provide the environment information needed to rebuild them. More broadly, basic descriptive metadata (creators, a version stamp) can almost always be recovered automatically, whereas the deeper information required for genuine reproduction is frequently absent, which is precisely the asymmetry the study sets out to quantify.

**Automated extraction is feasible but remains a bottleneck.** Benchmarked against the human-verified ground truth, the strongest open-weight model attains an entity-level F1 of approximately 0.27 on the reproducibility fields under entity-aware matching, with the larger model outperforming the smaller ones. Accuracy is highest for datasets and lowest for reported evaluation results, which are typically buried in tables that resist reliable extraction. These figures establish that populating a reproducibility graph from paper text with current open models is possible but imperfect, and they motivate SRAF's fusion of deterministic repository parsing with model-based extraction, together with human verification of the resulting benchmark. During construction of the ground truth we additionally identified and corrected a number of annotation errors; an independent verification confirms that 96.9% of ground-truth entities are grounded in their source.

## 6  Discussion

The results support three claims. First, computational reproducibility in machine learning is low even under a generous, version-aware reconstruction protocol, and the gap is driven less by missing basic metadata than by missing deep, executable detail. Second, that gap is measurable: metadata completeness, and in particular the presence of simple hygiene signals such as a license, is statistically associated with reproducibility, and a standards-grounded score can capture it. Third, automated construction of the underlying graph is within reach but not solved, so a fused deterministic-and-model pipeline with human oversight is currently the pragmatic design.

SRAF has limitations. Reproduction is assessed at the level of environment reconstruction rather than full re-execution of every experiment, so a repository counted as "built" is not thereby shown to reproduce its published numbers; result-level reproduction is ongoing work. Extraction accuracy limits the completeness of the paper-derived portion of the graph, and the ground truth, though human-verified, was drawn from a single annotation protocol. Finally, the corpus, while diverse, is a sample of machine-learning research and the findings should be generalised with care.

## 7  Conclusion

We presented SRAF, a standards-grounded framework for auditing the reproducibility of machine-learning research over a research-provenance knowledge graph. Its core is FAIR-R, a graded scoring instrument that inherits its criteria and weights from the RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model, F-UJI, FAIR4RS, and the ML Reproducibility Checklist, and extends FAIR with a Reproducible dimension expressed as executable SHACL shapes. Applied to 96 machine-learning repositories with actual environment reconstruction, SRAF shows that reproduction is difficult, that a simple metadata signal, the software license, significantly predicts it, and that a standards-grounded completeness score carries outcome-relevant signal. SRAF turns reproducibility from a qualitative aspiration into a measurable, actionable property, and its annotated corpus and instrument are released to support further study. Ongoing work extends the framework to result-level reproduction and to a deeper analysis of automated extraction.
