# N9 — Inter-annotator agreement on the ground-truth corpus

## Why this is not optional

Every F1 number in the paper is measured against `data/ground_truth/`. Those files
say, in their own metadata:

```
_verified_by:          "First Author"   (all 107 files)
_verification_depth:   thorough 24, standard 83
```

The corpus was generated by Gemini 2.5 Flash from the paper PDF plus
`repo_metadata.json`, then verified by one person. A reviewer does not have to
dig for this: it is recorded in the released artifact.

The objection is not "you were careless". It is structural. When the same person
builds the measuring instrument, defines what counts as a correct extraction, and
decides which of the model's proposals are right, the reported F1 partly measures
that person's judgement rather than a property of the artifacts. That is exactly
the inference the paper's thesis rests on.

This is a sharper problem than the taxonomy coding (N6). The taxonomy explains
26 failures; the ground truth underwrites every number in the paper.

## Two studies, one recruit

| | N9 — corpus ground truth | N6 — failure taxonomy |
|---|---|---|
| unit | extracted item (7 fields) | failure case |
| size | 96 studies, thousands of items | 26 cases |
| what it defends | Table 5 and all F1 figures | the taxonomy section |
| effort | ~8 h at the sample size below | ~2 h |

Recruit one person for both. It is the same skill (reading an ML paper and its
repository), the codebooks are short, and asking twice costs more goodwill than
asking once for a day and a half.

## Sampling

Full double annotation is out of reach: 96 studies times 7 fields, at 20–30
minutes per study, is 40+ hours. Nobody expects it, and reviewers do not ask for
it. What they ask for is a defensible subsample.

**Take the 12 gold-tier studies in full.**

They are the headline tier, so full coverage there means the strongest claims
rest on doubly-verified data, and taking a tier whole needs no justification of
which members were drawn.

That is 12.5% of the corpus and 1,127 items. The time estimate is 6 to 9
hours, widened on 9 Nov 2026: the closest comparable study reports 40 minutes per
publication for coding ten binary variables, and our unit is a study of roughly
94 candidate rows each needing a keep/discard/unsure decision and sometimes a
note, so the earlier figure scaled from an internal guess was optimistic. Tell
the annotator the range, not a point estimate. Fix the sample with a recorded seed and commit the study list
*before* annotation starts, so it cannot be read as chosen after the fact.

**Revision, 9 Nov 2026.** The original design added 8 silver-tier studies drawn
at random and stratified across the FAIR-R score bands, for 20 studies and 1,627
items. Those 8 were cut to fit the annotation budget. The cut is by tier and not
by draw, so no study was selected or dropped on anything observed about it, and
`evaluation/annotation_round1/SAMPLE.json` is left untouched as the record of
what was originally drawn under seed 20261009. Two consequences to state in the
paper: the agreement estimate now covers the gold tier only and is not claimed
for silver, and the silver tier carried 17 of the 27 `random_seeds` items, so
that field drops to 10 and leaves the agreement study altogether. See also the
per-field counts below.

**Per-field item counts on the 12 gold studies:** methods 349, evaluation_results
220, dependencies 216, hyperparameters 159, datasets 143, environment 30,
random_seeds 10. Report a per-field Cohen's kappa for the first five only.
`environment` and `random_seeds` are below the threshold at which a coefficient
means anything; report raw agreement counts for those two with no kappa, as
established for N5.

**Calibration, not screening (9 Nov 2026).** The ten codebook items in the
workbook were originally a qualification test with a pass mark of 8 of 10. They
are now training with recorded first answers: the codebook's answer and its
rationale are revealed as soon as the annotator answers, and nothing is gated on
the score. Three reasons. With a single second annotator a pass mark would never
have been enforced, so it was not a gate; the reference answers are embedded in
the workbook the annotator receives, so it was never a closed-book test either;
and the comparable literature finds that first-pass agreement is moved by
refining definitions rather than by filtering annotators (Ahmed et al.,
`10.7717/peerj-cs.2618`, reached Cohen's kappa 0.57 before refining theirs, see
paper_notes Y.5). The declared five-criterion qualification is unchanged and
still gates participation. Report the calibration score as a figure, stating
that it was not used to include or exclude anyone.

## What to measure, and why plain Cohen's kappa is the wrong instrument

Annotation here is **set-valued extraction**, not classification. Each study
yields a set of software, datasets, methods, parameters, seeds, environment facts
and evaluation results. Cohen's kappa needs a fixed set of items each rater
labels, and it needs a meaningful chance-agreement baseline. Neither holds when
annotators can add items nobody proposed. Reporting a single kappa over
set-valued data invites a methods reviewer to reject the statistic itself.

Report two things instead, which together cover both halves of the task:

**1. Agreement on the verification decisions — Cohen's kappa, legitimately.**
The corpus was built by verifying Gemini's proposals, so for every proposed item
there was a genuine binary decision: keep or discard. That *is* a fixed item set
with two labels, so kappa is well defined. Report it per field.

**2. Agreement on the final item sets — pairwise F1.**
An annotator can also add an item the model missed, which the kappa above cannot
see. Treat one annotator's set as the reference and score the other against it,
using the same lenient and strict matchers `eval_extraction.py` already
implements. This is the standard agreement measure in information extraction, and
reusing the existing matcher means annotator agreement and model performance are
measured on exactly the same definition of "correct" — which is the detail that
makes the comparison meaningful.

Report both per field. Expect them to diverge: high kappa with mediocre F1 means
you agree on the model's proposals but differ on what it missed, which is itself
a finding about where extraction is hard.

## Protocol

1. **Write the codebook first.** One page: what counts as a software dependency,
   when a version is "specified", how to treat `"exact version not specified"`,
   whether a metric in a related-work table counts. Most disagreement in this kind
   of task is definitional, and a codebook written after seeing the disagreements
   is not a codebook.
2. **Annotate blind.** The second annotator sees the paper PDF, the
   `repo_metadata.json`, and the Gemini candidates — never the existing verified
   file. Showing them the first annotation converts the exercise into a review and
   inflates agreement.
3. **Freeze, then compare.** Both sets are committed before any comparison is run.
4. **Adjudicate openly.** Disagreements are resolved jointly and the resolution is
   recorded per item, with the reason. Report how many items were adjudicated and
   in whose favour — a study where one annotator always wins reads as supervision,
   not agreement.
5. **Record the second annotator** in `_verified_by`, and add
   `_adjudicated: true` to the files that went through it.

## The part that carries real risk

Adjudication will change some ground-truth items, and those items feed Table 5.
**The F1 numbers can move.** Plan for that rather than discovering it in December:

- Re-run the evaluation on the corrected corpus and report the corrected figures
  as primary.
- Report the pre- and post-adjudication numbers side by side. A small shift that
  you surface yourself is a strength; the same shift found by a reviewer is a
  correction notice.
- Pre-commit now to reporting whichever direction it moves. The same discipline
  the plan already applies to N3.

If the corrected numbers move materially, that is a finding worth stating
plainly: it would mean single-annotator ground truth is itself a source of
measurement error in reproducibility auditing, which is on-thesis rather than an
embarrassment.

## Effort and placement

| step | who | time |
|---|---|---|
| write codebook, fix sample with a seed | you | 3 h |
| build the blind annotation bundles | you | 2 h (scripted) |
| second annotation, 12 gold studies | second annotator | 6-9 h |
| taxonomy coding, 26 cases (N6) | same person | 2 h |
| agreement computation | you | 2 h (scripted) |
| adjudication | both | 3 h |
| re-run evaluation, write up | you | 4 h |

Roughly two days of your time and a day and a half of theirs.

**The binding constraint is the person, not the work.** Everything above is
schedulable except finding them, and that has already taken weeks. Until someone
has said yes with a date, N6 and N9 are both at risk, and they are the two items
a reviewer is most likely to demand rather than merely prefer.

Because the corrected corpus feeds the evaluation, this has to finish before the
final numbers are frozen — which puts the annotation itself in the first half of
November, not December.
